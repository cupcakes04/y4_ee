A2 QR lab

Solving Ax = b is hard. Solving a triangular system is easy: start at the bottom row and substitute upwards. So multiply both sides by orthogonal matrices (Givens rotations or Householder mirrors) until A becomes triangular. x doesn't change, because both sides got the same treatment.

Source: EEEE3116 A2 “What are the nice matrices?”, sl.2–3, 12–24, 33–47, 51–68, plus the Givens and Householder practicals. Anything not from the slides is tagged added.

1 · A Givens rotation is a 2D rotation of two rows

A Givens rotation only touches two rows, i and k. Each column of those two rows is a pair of numbers, i.e. a 2D arrow. G turns every arrow by the same angle. Pick the angle so that the pivot column’s arrow lies flat on the axis. Then its bottom number, the entry you want gone, is 0.

pivot column’s pair (pivot, target) the other columns of the same two rows

Predict first: where must the orange arrow point for its bottom number to be 0? How long will it be then? Turn the dial, then press “snap”.

Two ways of writing the same G. The lecture writes [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]] and solves for θ (sl.14). The practical writes [[c, s], [−s, c]] with c = a/r, s = b/r and never computes θ. They are the same matrix with s = −sin θ.

    3 · Householder is a mirror: one reflection clears a whole column

    Givens turns the column bit by bit. Householder reflects it straight onto the axis in one go. Reflections keep length too, so the target is x′ = (±|x|, 0, …). The mirror sits halfway between x and x′. Its normal is u = x − x′, and H = I − 2uuᵀ/uᵀu. Drawn here in 2D: the practical’s second step, x = (1.871, −3.742).

    x (sub-column) x′ = Hx (on the axis) u = x − x′ and the mirror

    Predict first: turn x close to the positive axis (θ ≈ 5°) and switch to “same as x₀”. How long is u now? Why might a computer dislike that?

    One Givens kills one entry. One Householder kills a whole column below the diagonal. Sl.47: Householder is “very efficient … widely used (preferred)”.

    4 · Rank: a zero on R’s diagonal (sl.53, 65–68)

    Sl.68’s matrix has row 2 = 2 × row 1: the same equation written twice. Here row 2 is (8, 16, 4 + ε). At ε = 0 it is exactly sl.68’s matrix. R comes from Householder QR, computed live. b = (11, 14, b₂) added: b chosen so x = (1, 1, 1) works at ε = 0, b₂ = 28

    Predict first: slide ε toward 0. Which entry of R shrinks? Then switch b₂ to 28.1, a tiny error in b, and slide ε toward 0 again. What happens to x?

    Each 0 on R’s diagonal behaves like one equation removed. A 3×3 with one zero is really rank 2 (sl.67). Back-substitution would divide by that 0. On a computer it shows up as a tiny number instead, so you threshold it (“ignore if below threshold”, sl.67). This is the same failure as a zero k in A3 and a zero σ in A4.

    5 · Non-square: too many equations, or too few (sl.55–62)

    Tall (more equations than unknowns): best fit. Six points, one line y = m·t + c: 6 equations, 2 unknowns, so no exact solution. QR’s top 2 rows give m and c. The bottom 4 rows of Qᵀb are what no line can fit, and their length equals the misfit. added: example points

    Wide (fewer equations): smallest solution. One equation, two unknowns: 3x₀ + 4x₁ = β. Every point on the line is a solution. QR on Aᵀ sets the free part to 0, and that gives the point closest to the origin, the smallest-norm x (sl.61–62). added: 2D version of sl.62’s 3D picture
