A5 iterative lab

Real engineering matrices are huge and sparse: each node only talks to its neighbours. Multiplying such a matrix is cheap. Factorising it (QR, LU) fills the zeros in and runs out of memory. So A5 solves Ax = b using only multiplies: guess, measure the miss, adjust, repeat.

Source: EEEE3116 A5 “Why are zeros good and why so many of them?”, sl.3–55, plus the iterative practical (A = [2 1; 1 3], b = [1; 2]). Anything not from the slides is tagged added. Worked numbers: A5_iterative.md.

The answer x is the same either way: renumbering just reorders the equations (sl.41). The work changes. Row-by-row numbering keeps every non-zero within n of the diagonal (a band, sl.40). LU then fills in only inside that band. Random numbering scatters the entries, LU fills in nearly everything, and you're back to dense storage. At N = 10⁸ that's 160 PB (sl.11). QR is worse still: Q comes out dense in general (sl.10). sl.41 adds that silly numbering also costs accuracy, because of more rounding.

d is computed each step, not tuned. It is the bottom of the bowl along the line. MRA takes the lowest ‖r‖ on the line (sl.27). SD takes the lowest point of the energy bowl, a close cousin whose downhill direction is exactly r added. Too small wastes the step. Past twice the best value, the error ends up bigger than where you started: that's your overshoot and divergence intuition. A fixed step would need tuning like a gain. Computing d each time avoids that, and it's why SD is guaranteed to converge for symmetric positive definite A (sl.25).

4 · Jacobi vs Gauss-Seidel: one equation at a time (sl.33)

Each equation is a line. Row 0, 2x₀ + x₁ = 1, solved for x₀ means: keep x₁ and slide sideways onto line 0. Row 1, x₀ + 3x₁ = 2, solved for x₁ means: keep x₀ and slide up or down onto line 1. The answer is where the lines cross.

Reveal

Gauss-Seidel is a staircase: across to line 0, then up to line 1, so r₁ = 0 after every sweep. It reaches the crossing faster (×1/6 per sweep here, against Jacobi's ×0.41 added). Jacobi computes both slides from the old point, then jumps diagonally to the corner. It's slower, but both rows can run at the same time on separate CPUs. With the order swapped, the diagonal (1 and 1) is small next to the off-diagonals (3 and 2). The staircase then walks away from the crossing. Being diagonally dominant, where each diagonal is bigger than the rest of its row, is what keeps these splits safe. It's the same property that makes CG fast (sl.31) added.

Reveal: why CG finishes in 2

SD always heads straight downhill. Each new direction is at 90° to the last (r₁ ⟂ r₀), so in a stretched bowl it keeps undoing part of its own progress: the zig-zag. CG mixes in some of the old direction (+β·p) so that the new direction is A-conjugate to the old one, pᵀ₀Ap₁ = 0. Tick “round the bowl”. In coordinates where the bowl is a circle, CG's two directions are simply perpendicular, and two perpendicular moves finish a 2D problem. In general that's ≤ N moves for N unknowns (sl.30). In practice rounding breaks this on big problems (sl.30). added picture

Reveal: preconditioning

Stretching raises κ, and SD slows down badly (6 → 23 iterations at s = 20). Jacobi actually speeds up, because a bigger 2s makes A more diagonally dominant (panel 4). CG still finishes in 2, but in big problems rounding stops it hitting its N-step finish (sl.30). A preconditioner Z ≈ A⁻¹ turns the problem into ZAx = Zb with ZA ≈ I (sl.32). ZA ≈ I means a round bowl, where downhill points straight at the answer, so one or two steps do it (sl.31). Z = D⁻¹ (divide each row by its diagonal) is the cheapest guess. sl.33 says Jacobi “can be used as a preconditioner”. Here it undoes the 2s stretch almost completely. Finding a good Z for a real problem is “those other 55 credits” (sl.32). Rule of thumb: no strong convergence in ~100 iterations means it never will, so preconditioning is critical (sl.34).

6 · Domain decomposition: where the parallel speed-up comes from (sl.47–55)

sl.50’s thermal grid, cut into domains a, b and c by an interface. Domains never touch each other directly, only through the interface. Number each domain’s nodes together and the interface last, and A becomes the block shape of sl.51–52: [Ã B̃; C̃ D̃].

In domain order, Ã is block-diagonal, one block per domain. LU of Ã fills in only inside each block, so CPU a, CPU b and CPU c each factorise and solve their own block, all at once (sl.54). The blocks never mix. The only dense piece is the bottom-right corner, the Schur complement D̃ − C̃Ã⁻¹B̃ (sl.52). It is m × m (interface size), far smaller than N × N. Ordinary LU (sl.48) can't be split this way: row k of the result needs the finished rows above it, so the work is sequential. Matrix multiply is the easy case to parallelise, because each row of y is independent (sl.47). That's another reason iterative methods suit big clusters.
