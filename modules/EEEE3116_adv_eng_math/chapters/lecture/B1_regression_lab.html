B1 regression, covariance and rotation lab

One dataset, three views. Fit a line by making the squared residuals small. Measure how two variables move together (covariance, then the unit-free ρ). Rotate the axes so one coordinate carries nearly all the variance, which is the A3 eigen problem on the matrix S.

Source: EEEE3116 B1 “Linear regression, covariance and axis rotation”, sl.8–32, 41–50, 57, 65–69, and the Section B practical (Q2, Q3). Anything not from the slides is tagged added.

Fit: rᵢ = yᵢ − (c + m xᵢ),  SSres = Σ rᵢ²,  X v = y is tall (N×2) → XᵀX v = Xᵀy,  r² = 1 − SSres/SStot sl.12–20, 30
Together: cov(x,y) = (1/N) Σ (xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ),  cov(x,x) = σₓ²,  ρ = cov / (σₓσᵧ),  S = (1/N) AᵀA sl.42–48
Rotate: [x′; y′] = R [x; y], R = [cos θ sin θ; −sin θ cos θ],  Sᴿ = R S Rᵀ,  trace Sᴿ = trace S sl.66–67
Best θ: tan 2θ = 2 cov / (σₓ² − σᵧ²)  ⇔  x′ axis = top eigenvector of S,  variances on the new axes = eigenvalues sl.69

check

Flat at ȳ means SSres = SStot, so r² = 0: the line explains nothing that “always guess ȳ” didn't (sl.31). A line worse than that gives r² < 0. The slide's 0 ≤ r² ≤ 1 holds for the least-squares line, which is never worse than the flat one. added

Why the 5 equations can't all be met: X (5×2) has rank 2, so its columns are fine. The noise makes the 5 equations disagree with each other. Multiplying by Xᵀ gives the 2×2 system XᵀX v = Xᵀy, which is A2's tall, best-fit case (A2 sl.57). XᵀX is exactly the normal-equation matrix [N Σx; Σx Σx²] (sl.20).

2 · Walking downhill (gradient descent)

Left: c is held at 2.01 and only m moves (sl.25–27). Right: c and m move together on the 2-D bowl (sl.28), using the raw x versus the z-scored x.

1-D: m only, c = 2.01

Near the bottom, the slope is 396(m − m*). One step does (m − m*) ← (1 − 396μ)(m − m*). So |1 − 396μ| < 1 is the stability condition, giving μ < 2/396 ≈ 0.0051 (sl.27). This is the discrete version of “the pole must be inside the unit circle”. added: the pole framing

2-D: c and m together

Both views start from the same line; they only label it with different parameters. Raw x gives a long, thin valley: the step that is safe across the valley is tiny along it, so the path zig-zags and crawls. z-scoring x makes XᵀX = N·I, a round bowl. added: the step counts and μ/limit scale

With z-scored x, set μ/limit to 0.5. How many steps?
check

One. In a round bowl the gradient points straight at the bottom, and μ = 1/λ lands on it. That's why sl.28 recommends z-scoring first, and why A5's CG beats steepest descent.

3 · Covariance as rectangles; ρ as the unit-free version

Each point draws a rectangle from the mean point (x̄, ȳ) to itself. Its signed area is (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ): green = + (both above or both below the mean), red = −. cov = the average signed area. cov(x, x) uses squares, so it's σₓ².

Practical Q3: PM2.5–traffic cov = 25, PM2.5–wind cov = −2. Is wind the weak link? Switch between the two and compare ρ.
check

No. Wind's σ is only 0.71 m/s, so its covariances are small numbers because of its units. ρ(PM, wind) = −0.58, a moderate negative link (more wind, less PM). Covariance alone can't tell you “strong”; ρ can, because it divides the units out.

Try circle and parabola: y depends completely on x. What's ρ? Then try one outlier.
check

ρ ≈ 0 for both: the green and red rectangles cancel. ρ only measures a linear link (sl.50). One far point makes a huge rectangle and fakes a strong ρ.

ρ² equals r² of the least-squares line through the same points (sl.49). The readout computes r² separately from the residuals, so you can check this.

4 · Rotate the axes (and why the answer is an eigenvector)

Data with the means removed. The rotation doesn't move the points; it changes the axes we read them in (sl.66). Each point's x′ is its shadow on the x′ axis, and its y′ is the perpendicular stick.

data:
Before you move θ: at which angle does the covariance between x′ and y′ hit 0? Is it the same angle where the variance on x′ peaks?
check

The same angle, and not by luck. Differentiate σ²ₓ′(θ) from sl.69: dσ²ₓ′/dθ = 2·cov′(θ). So the peak of the variance is exactly where cov′ = 0. Setting it to 0 gives sl.69's tan 2θ = 2cov/(σₓ² − σᵧ²). added: the derivative step

The A3 view: S acting on u

u = x′ direction (×λ₀)SuS·(unit circle)

Why the eigenvector. Write u = (cos θ, sin θ) for the x′ direction and v = (−sin θ, cos θ) for y′. These are R's two rows. Then the entries of Sᴿ = R S Rᵀ are just dot products with Su:

σ²ₓ′ = u·(Su),   cov′ = v·(Su).

cov′ = 0 means Su has no part along v, so Su points along u: Su = λu. That's A3's definition of an eigenvector, and then σ²ₓ′ = u·λu = λ. In eigen axes, Sᴿ = K is diagonal. S is symmetric, so its eigen axes are perpendicular, and R is a proper rotation (A3: S = X K Xᵀ, with R = Xᵀ). added: the u·Su / v·Su framing

Regression angle vs eigen angle: 41.4° vs 41.5° on the circuit data. Regression minimises the vertical sticks, and the eigenvector minimises the perpendicular ones (sl.69). Tick the box to see both.

Careful with R: sl.66's R = [cos sin; −sin cos] rotates the axes by +θ. A1's R = [cos −sin; sin cos] rotates points. They are transposes of each other: turning the axes by +θ looks like turning the points by −θ.
